Surgeon saves overseas worker's severed thumb, 40 hours after accident.

With nearly 1,000 finger reattachment surgeries under his belt, surgeon Wei Qingyu had never doubted the chances of success in the operating theater — until he was presented with a thumb that had been severed for 40 hours.

The case involved a worker surnamed Wang, who got injured at a construction site in Libya on Sept 7, when a steel wire rope suddenly sprang up and severed his left thumb.

Local medical institutions could not perform the replantation, so he had to return to China for treatment.

He first flew to Turkiye, then to Shanghai and from there caught another flight to Chengdu, capital of Sichuan province. At around 3 am on Sept 9, Wang finally arrived at No 1 Orthopedics Hospital of Chengdu.

"The patient's left thumb was severed at the base, with only a small amount of skin still attached. The blood vessels were completely ruptured, and the bone was fractured in two places," said Wei at the hospital's repair and reconstruction surgery department.

"The golden period for replantation is only six to eight hours. The longer the delay, the exponentially higher the risk of necrosis in blood vessels, nerves and muscles," he said, adding that a 40-hour post-detachment period presented challenges.

Such a lengthy period is rare in replantation cases both at home and abroad. According to Wei, the longest case the hospital had previously handled was under 30 hours.

Despite nearly nine years of experience in replantation surgery, handling more than 100 cases a year, Wei admitted he could not guarantee success in Wang's case.

"In fact, the patient also had little confidence in replantation himself," Wei said. "He told us that he came to the hospital prepared for thumb reconstruction."

Thumb reconstruction refers to building a new thumb using materials from elsewhere on the body, such as skin, nail and joints from a toe.

"The original thumb is always the best. So after discussing it with the patient, we decided to try replantation first, and turn to reconstruction if it failed," Wei said.

Upon Wang's admission, the hospital immediately opened a green channel for emergency treatment. The medical team from the repair and reconstruction surgery department sprinted into action, while the anesthesia and operating center units simultaneously made preparations, ensuring a seamless treatment process.

"Even beyond the golden period for replantation, as long as there is a glimmer of hope, we will not easily give up," said Zhou Mengsen, director of the department.

Replantation requires surgeons to work under a microscope, reattaching blood vessels and nerves so fine that some are thinner than a strand of human hair, along with tendons and bone. Wang's case was even more challenging, as the prolonged detachment had caused severe necrosis in the severed thumb's blood vessels, according to the medical team.

The operation, which lasted nearly three hours, was a success.

"Normally, once the artery is reconnected, the thumb would regain color within two or three seconds. In this case, it took about half a minute before blood flow gradually resumed," Wei said.

The first seven to 10 days after surgery are considered a high-risk period for vascular spasms, thrombosis and infection. Medical personnel monitored Wang closely, watching for changes in the thumb's blood supply and fine-tuning his treatment plan.

"The thumb has fully survived," Wei said more than a month after the surgery, adding that he expects its function to recover to 80 to 90 percent of normal within a year or two.
