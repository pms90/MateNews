Miao, Dong wisdom hailed as viable model for climate solutions.

Ancient wisdom from the Miao and Dong ethnic communities in Southwest China can offer practical lessons for building resilient societies and advancing global climate action, according to speakers addressing a side event at the China Pavilion during the recently concluded COP31.

The event, themed "Ancient Wisdom for Modern Challenges", also saw the release of a report titled Miao and Dong Wisdom for Climate Challenges, on the sidelines of the 31st session of the Conference of the Parties to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, which concluded on Friday in Antalya, Turkiye.

Zhang Haibin, an associate dean and professor at Peking University's School of International Studies, said global climate governance had entered a stage of full implementation since the adoption of the landmark 2015 Paris Agreement.

"At this stage, we face a very practical challenge: Can well-intentioned policies and technologies truly take root when they reach local communities?" Zhang said.

He said the question was not only about finance and technology but also about how governance could adapt to local society, culture and ecology.

"The report on Miao and Dong villages released today offers a valuable, grounded sample in this regard," Zhang said. "Communities possess their own wisdom and resilience in withstanding climate risks."

The report's findings were presented by Wang Binbin, founding director of Climate Future Global Innovation Lab, or C Force Lab, and associate research professor at Peking University's Institute for Carbon Neutrality.

"When people ask me why C Force Lab spent a year on this report, the answer is a question we kept asking: What survives when the inputs stop?" Wang said.

She cited examples from Qiandongnan Miao and Dong autonomous prefecture in Guizhou province. During a severe drought in 2022, hybrid rice lost more than 40 percent of its harvest, while traditional rice in Huanggang village's rice-fish-duck paddies remained productive.

Two years later, 45 days of heavy rain caused severe soil erosion, but the area's old stone terraces did not collapse, Wang said.

"Neither result came from outside investment. They came from systems designed to work without them," she said.

Wang said the communities offered new ways to consider three long-standing climate questions: emissions, adaptation and finance.

The traditional rice-fish-duck system, fermented sour soup and traditional crafts require no chemical fertilizers, industrial processing or cold chains, she said. "Emissions are not cut afterward; they are never created."

Adaptation, she said, was embedded in local culture, with forests protected as ancestors and centuries-old granaries designed to resist moisture and fire.

"Finance can be generated from within," Wang said, citing women's cooperatives that reinvest in native seeds as an example. Such internal cycles cannot replace international finance, she said, but they can help ensure that basic ecological care does not depend entirely on external pledges.

Zhang Guodan, a woman of the Dong ethnic group who performed traditional songs at the side event, said, "For generations, our ancestors have passed down a farming culture shaped by high mountains, cold water and traditional rice cultivation."

"Some people see these practices as outdated and believe they should be replaced by greater mechanization. But we use this traditional wisdom to enrich and protect the soil," she said.

She said the communities stored water throughout the year, maintained rich microbial ecosystems and carefully protected mountain forests.

"It is a complete system," she said.

Attending COP31 and sharing the wisdom of China's traditional cultures with the world moved Zhang deeply.

"I was moved to tears by the applause," she said.

Liu Yang, deputy director of the Ministry of Ecology and Environment's department of climate change, said China's long history and diverse traditions had created a rich source of environmental wisdom.

"Chinese civilization has always valued the unity of humanity and nature and followed the laws of nature, pursuing harmony between humanity and nature," Liu said.

She added that climate solutions required both advanced science and engineering, and a renewed understanding of traditional knowledge.

"The convergence of traditional wisdom and modern science will inject deeper and more lasting momentum into the global green and low-carbon transition," Liu said.

Camilla Nordheim-Larsen of UNESCO said culture and climate change had long been treated as separate issues, but that approach was no longer sufficient.

"Culture is not only a victim of climate change. It is also a source of solutions," she said.

The wisdom of Miao and Dong communities, she added, was "not a relic of the past", but "a resource for the future".

When the Dong Grand Song is performed, knowledge of seasons, farming and the relationship between people and nature is passed from one generation to the next.
