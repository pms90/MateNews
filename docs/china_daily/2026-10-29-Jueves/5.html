Archaeological sites brought closer to the public.

At the Dahecun National Archaeological Site Park in Zhengzhou, Central China's Henan province, reeds sway in the autumn breeze and deer can be heard calling across the nearly 160-hectare landscape.

The new Dahecun Site Museum within the park is built largely underground, with a sloping entrance leading visitors into the history of a Neolithic settlement dating back about 7,000 years.

"This is the country's first thematic museum to present the Yangshao culture in a panoramic form," said Wang Xiaoya, a guide at the museum. "Its rectangular roof is level with the ground, allowing the building to blend into the landscape like an archaeological site emerging from the soil."

Inside, more than 1,500 cultural relics illustrate the Yangshao culture and shed light on the history of the Dahecun site. Outside, the core protection zone displays the remains of a large Yangshao-period settlement. Offering an important window into a Neolithic culture that lasted about 2,000 years, Dahecun is one of China's major archaeological sites, where protection and public presentation are making the traces of ancient life more accessible to the public.

Known in Chinese as "dayizhi", these archaeological sites include the remains of major ancient settlements, cities, palaces and tombs, as well as related groups of sites and cultural landscapes. They carry historical and cultural information across different stages of ancient Chinese history, and are distinguished by their scale, historical and cultural significance, and far-reaching influence.

The category includes sites such as Liangzhu in East China's Zhejiang province, the Yinxu Ruins in Henan, Sanxingdui in the southwestern province of Sichuan, the Old Summer Palace ruins in Beijing, the Xixia Imperial Tombs and the Great Wall. These sites attest to China's million-year human history, 10,000-year cultural history and more than 5,000-year-old civilization.

China now has 150 such archaeological sites. A total of 65 national archaeological site parks have been established based on some of these sites, incorporating the surrounding areas.

Luoyang, a city in Henan with 4,000 years of urban history and more than 1,500 years as a capital, is home to several major archaeological sites that are closely woven into the modern cityscape.

"Several major archaeological sites lie within an east-west stretch of less than 50 kilometers along the Luo River, including Erlitou, the Yanshi Shang Dynasty Capital Ruins, the Han-Wei Luoyang Ancient City site, the Sui-Tang Luoyang City site and the Mangshan tombs," said Zhao Xiaojun, head of the Luoyang municipal cultural heritage bureau.

"The density, scale, time span and richness of historical content are rare in the world."

As early as the 1950s, Luoyang developed a new urban area away from the old city and required archaeological excavations before construction began. Authorities have adopted site-specific regulations to protect these archaeological sites. The Sui-Tang Luoyang City site, the Han-Wei Luoyang Ancient City site and Erlitou have all become national archaeological site parks and cultural landmarks woven into city life.

Nationwide, more people are visiting such places. According to statistics, visits to China's national archaeological site parks rose from 20.97 million in 2021 to 97.74 million in 2025, bringing the five-year total to 303 million.

Their benefits also extend beyond archaeological research and museum exhibitions. In Zhejiang, efforts to link Liangzhu culture with rural vitalization have supported local economic development.

The operating income of village collectives in 14 villages within the site area rose 19 percent year-on-year in 2025. At the Liulihe site in Beijing, a China-United States international field archaeology summer school offers young people from around the world a platform to learn about Chinese civilization.

China's 15th Five-Year Plan (2026-30) for the development of national cultural heritage calls for systematic protection and utilization of such sites.

A recent national conference on work related to major archaeological sites stressed the need for high-level archaeological research, sustainable protection and efficient management, while also calling for high-quality presentation.

For sites in city centers, small-scale exhibition spaces will be integrated into the urban fabric, while sites in suburban and rural areas can explore models that combine rural revitalization with educational field trips and study programs.

Efforts will also be made to improve the cultural services provided by these sites, better realize the value of cultural heritage and meet public needs.

For visitors, these sites offer a more direct encounter with the past.
