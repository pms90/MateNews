DPP targets political rivals ahead of elections.

Island authorities accused of using judicial system to attack opponents

Editor's note: The Taiwan question is a key focus for China and the international community. China Daily is publishing a series of reports to track hot Taiwan-related topics and address disinformation from the Democratic Progressive Party administration.

Taiwan's Democratic Progressive Party authorities have faced criticism over recent attempts to pursue legal action and investigations against opposition figures, with critics saying the moves serve the authorities' political interests and could create a "chilling effect" in Taiwan.

Ahead of Taiwan's "nine-in-one" local elections on Saturday, which will determine nine categories of local public officials, including city mayors, county magistrates and councilors, several lawmakers and councilors outside the DPP have faced indictments or other legal action.

Opposition parties and critics on the island have accused the DPP of undermining public confidence in the judiciary and compromising electoral fairness, describing the situation in Taiwan as "judicial persecution".

The Chinese mainland has also condemned the DPP for abusing judicial tools to suppress political opponents while deliberately restricting cross-Strait exchanges in pursuit of "Taiwan independence".

On Nov 6, Taiwan's high prosecutors office indicted independent lawmaker Kao Chin Su-mei and three others, accusing them of receiving some NT$170 million ($5.15 million) from the mainland between 2005 and 2016. The office sought a 14-year prison sentence for Kao, citing violations of the island's "security act".

Kao has denied the accusations, saying on Nov 11 that the indictment marked a point at which questions raised or positions expressed by lawmakers in Taiwan's legislature that run counter to the DPP authorities' policies could lead to indictments by prosecutors and investigators on allegations of endangering "Taiwan's security", even without direct evidence.

At a news conference on Nov 18, Chen Binhua, spokesman for the State Council Taiwan Affairs Office, denounced the DPP authorities' frequent fabrication of so-called communist espionage cases, accusing them of intensifying the "green terror" and unjustifiably framing people in Taiwan who support or participate in normal cross-Strait exchanges.

Chen said the DPP's approach seriously undermines the interests and well-being of Taiwan residents.

The timing of the indictment has also raised questions in Taiwan, as Kao's alleged offenses date back a decade but only came under public scrutiny ahead of this year's elections.

"Such blatant political persecution and an authoritarian approach under the banner of 'Taiwan independence' run counter to the shared aspirations of people on both sides of the Taiwan Strait for peace, development, exchanges and cooperation," Chen added.

Earlier, Chu Chen-yao, a Taoyuan councilor from the Chinese Kuomintang, was accused of fraudulently claiming more than NT$170,000 in legislative aide subsidies by using a fictitious aide. In the first-instance ruling on Oct 15, the Taoyuan district court sentenced her to eight years in prison and deprived her of civil rights for five years.

Cheng Li-wun, chairwoman of the KMT, strongly criticized the DPP for using the judicial system to suppress its opponents during the election period when she attended Chu's election campaign event in Taoyuan on Nov 1.

She said that after years in power, the DPP had little to show in terms of governance achievements or concrete policy results, and had instead taken the lead in stoking hatred and division.

"When faced with poor public sentiment ahead of the election, they resort to judicial and prosecutorial systems to create a chilling effect," Cheng said, accusing the DPP of disregarding judicial independence and impartiality.

On Nov 20, a female campaign aide to Hsu Hsin-ying, the KMT candidate for Hsinchu county magistrate, was questioned by prosecutors and police before being taken to the Hsinchu district prosecutors office for further questioning. Investigators also seized several computers from Hsu's campaign office.

Hsu and a large group of supporters gathered outside the prosecutors office on Friday evening to protest what they described as "judicial interference in the election".

Beyond officials, media commentators and entertainers in Taiwan have also faced legal action.

On Oct 22, Wu Tzu-chia, a political commentator, was accused of violating the "election and recall act" after exposing a DPP candidate's alleged tax evasion. Prosecutors and police also searched his home and office.

The DPP's use of judicial measures against political opponents has drawn criticism on the island.

Chen Kuo-hsiang, a media professional and former chairman of Taiwan's Central News Agency, wrote that "when politicians fear prolonged legal proceedings, media professionals worry about searches and prosecutions, and online content creators fear facing steep legal costs for criticizing the government, a climate of self-censorship may gradually take hold in society".

He said selective enforcement erodes public trust in the judiciary, while the accompanying invisible pressure subtly erodes the foundations of democracy.

Taiwan singer-songwriter Lo Ta-yu also criticized the DPP authorities publicly, saying in a radio interview on Nov 10, they had become a source of fear among Taiwan residents and contributed to a deterioration of the island's cultural environment.

"Even if I risk being arrested, I still have to say this," Lo, 72, said. The veteran singer urged people in Taiwan to "wake up to the situation and speak out for the next generation".

'Green justice'

The "anti-infiltration act", enacted in 2019, has been used to bring legal cases against political figures whom the DPP considers to have close ties with the Chinese mainland. Conduct punishable under the act includes accepting illegal political donations, interfering in election activities, engaging in unlawful lobbying and spreading false information to influence election outcomes.

Recently, Cheng Ming-chien, head of Taiwan's justice department, said the authorities had begun procedures to amend the act in response to what they described as "transnational repression", proposing to introduce additional criminal offenses and impose harsher penalties.

The weaponization of the judicial system and the selective use of legal cases to target political opponents is known as "green justice" in Taiwan, according to Chen Shiliang, secretary-general of the Institute of Taiwan Studies at East China Normal University in Shanghai.

Green is the color closely associated with the DPP in Taiwan. The DPP authorities have repeatedly used administrative, judicial and media resources at their disposal as campaign tools, turning public power into a means of electoral campaigning and public governance into a mechanism for supporting the party's election efforts, Chen said.

He described this as a long-standing problem in Taiwan's elections, saying it distorts the purpose of local elections and undermines the foundations of democracy.

Chen added that such practices not only erode public trust in government institutions but also deepen social divisions in Taiwan by heightening tensions among different political camps and diverting attention from livelihood issues.

Bao Chengke, a research fellow at the Shanghai Cross-Strait Research Association, said, "Social fairness and equality are being gradually eroded by Lai Ching-te-style authoritarianism and terror."

He said the DPP has been fostering "green terror" on the island by using political positions to draw lines in the judicial system. He cited the imprisonment of Ko Wen-je, former chairman of the Taiwan People's Party, as a typical example, arguing that Ko had been jailed without sufficient evidence being presented.

Ko, who won 26 percent of the vote in Taiwan's 2024 leadership election, was accused of involvement in a corruption case by the Taipei district prosecutors office after the election. On March 26, the Taipei district court sentenced him to 17 years in prison and stripped him of his civil rights for six years in its first-instance ruling.

Bao also cited the DPP-initiated "great recall" campaign last year, which sought to remove opposition lawmakers from office but ultimately failed. "How can Taiwan be a 'city upon a hill' under Lai's authoritarianism?" he added.

Chen said the DPP's actions did little to improve people's livelihoods or promote local development, while undermining peace and stability across the Taiwan Strait.

"Peaceful cross-Strait exchanges and a stable, prosperous life for the people are what Taiwan people truly aspire to," Chen added.
