Accelerate natl water diversion project, urge experts.

Experts have emphasized the urgent need to strengthen the national water network and called for accelerating the South-to-North Water Diversion Project, saying water resources in North China remain insufficient to meet the demands of economic growth despite increased rainfall and humidity linked to climate change.

The project, currently the world's largest water transfer project, aims to alleviate water shortages in northern China and support socioeconomic development by channeling water from the south to more arid northern areas, including Beijing and Tianjin, through its Eastern, Central and Western routes.

The Eastern and Central routes began construction in 2002 and 2003, respectively, and are now in operation. The Western route, however, remains in the feasibility study stage, with major controversies centering on the melting of glaciers and its impact on runoff, as well as the increasing humidity in northern provinces amid climate change.

The Western route is designed to supplement water resources in the upper reaches of the Yellow River and address drought and water shortages in Northwest China.

Zhang Jianyun, an academician of the Chinese Academy of Engineering, said the challenges facing the project need to be addressed scientifically.

"The northward shift of the rain belt, for example, is a temporary climate fluctuation associated with climate change. Northern China still struggles with inadequate water resources to meet its economic needs. Therefore, the South-to-North Water Diversion Project remains crucial, and its further development should be accelerated despite temporary increases in rainfall," he said at a forum on the construction of the national water network on Saturday.

He said the increasing humidity in Northwest China is the result of both cyclical changes and global warming, but is insufficient to resolve the region's water scarcity.

"The moistening trend is slowing and is unlikely to transform the region into a warm and humid climate in the near future. The region faces severe water shortages, threatening ecological security and development, and current measures cannot fundamentally solve the problem," he said, recommending that the Western route be accelerated to address these challenges.

Gao Shiji, a researcher at the Development Research Center of the State Council, said effective water governance is fundamental to the construction of an ecological civilization in China, and the South-to-North Water Diversion Project is of great importance to the country's long-term development and people's well-being.

He said climate change could amplify challenges to global water governance, including potentially greater disaster losses and shortages of infrastructure, early warning systems and post-disaster recovery funds in developing countries, which are needed to adapt to climate change.

"Against the backdrop of climate change, the government can strengthen institutional and legal frameworks, establish inclusive multihazard early warning systems and promote nature-based solutions," he said.

"The authorities should ensure people's access to affordable and equitable water supplies and adaptable infrastructure while maintaining effective market regulation," Gao said at the forum on Saturday.

Since the first phases of the Eastern and Central routes began operating in 2014, more than 92 billion cubic meters of water have been transferred to the north, providing a reliable, high-quality water supply to nearly 200 million people and significantly supporting GDP growth of more than 19 trillion yuan ($2.85 trillion) in the northern region, according to a press release issued by the China South-to-North Water Diversion Corporation on Saturday.
