The $1 Trump coins are here. Why spending them is legal but tricky.

President Trump in 2024 and the U.S. Mint's $1 Trump coin. Photo: Anna Moneymaker/Getty Images (left); U.S. Mint and Treasury on X (right)

The U.S. Mint began selling $1 Trump coins Wednesday.

Why it matters: The Trump coin sits in a rare middle ground: The Mint is promoting it as a collectible, but under federal law, it has a face value of $1 and can be used as real money.

Driving the news: The U.S. Mint says the Trump coins are now "in circulation, so check your pocket change."

The big picture: Trump's coin has raised legal questions over whether a sitting president can appear on U.S. money.

"Legal tender" refers to U.S. money that can pay debts, taxes, dues and public charges, according to federal law .

Yes, but: Legal tender doesn't guarantee every store will accept Trump coins. Federal law generally lets private businesses decide what forms of payment they'll accept.

Catch up quick: Since the Trump coin was announced, questions have emerged about its design and legality because some federal laws restrict living people's portraits on U.S. coins.

The other side: The Treasury Department argued in October 2025 that the coin can be made because the CCCRA does allow for "designs emblematic of the United States semiquincentennial."

Case in point: Calvin Coolidge, the 30th president, appeared alongside George Washington on a 1926 half-dollar celebrating America's 150th anniversary.

The bottom line: The Trump $1 coin is real money, but that doesn't mean it can be spent everywhere.
