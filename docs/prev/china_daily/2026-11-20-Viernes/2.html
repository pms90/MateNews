China taking active role in regional marine governance.

When all 21 APEC economies agreed earlier this month to adopt the region's first APEC Ocean Literacy Action Plan — drafted by China after six months of negotiations — it demonstrated how Beijing is increasingly setting the agenda and standards for ocean governance in the Asia-Pacific.

Released during World Ocean Week in Xiamen, Fujian province, just ahead of this week's APEC Economic Leaders' Meeting in Shenzhen, the plan marks the latest milestone in China's evolution from a passive participant in Asia-Pacific marine talks to a primary architect of regional environmental policy, technology transfer and coastal conservation.

"China is no longer only responding to existing agendas, but beginning to shape the direction of marine development in the region," said Chen Danhong, director-general of the Department of International Cooperation at China's Ministry of Natural Resources, describing the country's expanding role over the past decade.

When China joined APEC in 1991, the forum's focus was largely on trade liberalization and facilitation, with marine issues remaining more of a technical track than a strategic priority. China used the platform to learn international rules and support its domestic economic development.

That trajectory changed in 2011, when the APEC Ocean and Fisheries Working Group was launched as a core platform for coordinating marine policies and actions among economies.

That same year, the APEC Marine Sustainable Development Center was inaugurated in Xiamen, and China introduced the concept of the "blue economy" into APEC discourse, helping elevate marine sustainability from a side topic to a shared regional priority.

"The concept gave APEC marine cooperation a main axis — connecting scattered topics like fisheries, marine debris and conservation under one coherent framework," Chen said.

In 2014, APEC ocean ministers formally defined the blue economy as balancing ocean sustainability with economic growth, naming it a top priority alongside coastal conservation, food security and marine innovation. APEC leaders later wrote that definition into their summit declaration.

As APEC marine cooperation accelerated and became more institutionalized, China delivered public goods to support capacity-building across the region. These efforts included convening blue economy forums, marine spatial planning workshops and marine debris seminars, as well as spearheading the APEC Marine Sustainable Development Report, now in its third edition, to track regional progress toward the United Nations 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development.

On the ground, that capacity-building has increasingly focused on community-level conservation. In Shenzhen this July, a three-day APEC workshop on community-based conservation of long-distance migratory species brought together more than 200 participants from 13 economies. The meeting produced a joint toolkit for tracking sea turtle and whale migration corridors — work that materialized months later when three green sea turtles were released off Phuket, Thailand.

"Ocean literacy is not only about knowing marine ecosystems and marine life, but also about understanding how the ocean connects with the climate, social life and human development, and building the capacity to care for the ocean and take part in its protection," said Cong Bailin, a researcher at the First Institute of Oceanography of the Ministry of Natural Resources.

Fitted with satellite beacons by the First Institute of Oceanography in September, the turtles can be tracked by the public through a dedicated platform. The platform also enables coastal residents and patrol staff to upload on-site photos and alert relevant departments and rescue teams when they encounter injured or stranded species.

Cong's institute also organized online exchange activities for teachers and students from China and Thailand to discuss deep-sea ecosystems and finless porpoise conservation. In addition, experts visited Thai middle schools to bring professional knowledge and frontline practices into the classroom.

"Such practices help extend APEC marine cooperation from experts and management authorities to young people and community groups, broadening its social foundation," he said.

Elsewhere in Southeast Asia, in North Sulawesi, Indonesia, the latest monitoring data shows that the artificial bio-reefs deployed there in April have attracted substantial coral larval settlement and are thriving.

Researchers from China's Third Institute of Oceanography of the Ministry of Natural Resources installed the reefs alongside their Indonesian counterparts. Despite speaking different languages, a simple gesture was all it took when it was time to lower them into place.

"No words were needed. Protecting coral reefs is a shared language and wish," said Du Jianguo, deputy director of the APEC Marine Sustainable Development Center at the Third Institute of Oceanography, who led the restoration work. He added that the institute's reef restoration technology has also been deployed in the Red Sea, Sri Lanka, the Maldives and elsewhere.

Such cooperation reflects a shift across the Asia-Pacific, according to Du, where marine issues are moving from the margins to the center of the regional agenda.

Deng Aifang, an assistant researcher at the First Institute of Oceanography, said that shifting public attitudes is essential, but requires sustained, long-term effort.

Working with Jenderal Soedirman University in Indonesia, Deng's team spent a year progressing from field surveys of mangrove conservation and community needs to a series of environmental education activities and, ultimately, to local residents' active participation in demonstration projects.

"At first, when we talked with local people about mangroves, many did not understand their role in protecting coastlines, supporting fisheries and sustaining community livelihoods. To them, mangroves were just ordinary trees," Deng said.

"But after our continuous efforts, families began to take an active part in mangrove planting and protection. When they proudly received their certificates, we were also very proud of our own work," she added.

"Ocean literacy and public engagement are not a one-off task, nor should APEC cooperation be," said Deng. "The real measure is not how many documents we produce, but whether communities along the coast feel the difference."
