Free noodles stir debate in Nanjing.

A free vegetarian noodle shop in Nanjing, Jiangsu province, available to the elderly and gig workers, has sparked debate over individual charity and its impact on nearby businesses, with some restaurants reporting a loss of customers and others considering moving out.

The topic of the Zero-Yuan Noodle Shop drew at least 12 million views on Sina Weibo, a Chinese social media platform, as of Sunday.

The Zero-Yuan Noodle Shop, which opened its doors in October, serves vegetarian noodles free of charge to those in need, the Yangtse Evening Post reported in early November.

From the onset, the shop permitted everyone, with no restrictions on age or income, with the elderly and those working in the informal sector making up a large part of their customer base.

A customer surnamed Pan, who is job-hunting and has no fixed address, visited the shop twice, saying the noodles brought him immediate relief.

However, since Nov 14, after complaints from neighboring businesses, free meals are now only available to people aged 60 and above, people with disabilities, sanitation workers, couriers and food-delivery riders, the Yangtse Evening Post reported.

The owner, surnamed Zhang, told local media that he wanted to do some good with the shop. The shop's two outlets together serve about 3,000 bowls a day, Zhang said.

For nearby noodle restaurants, however, the free meals have affected business.

Huang Haoming, dean of the Hainan-based Asia Academy of Philanthropy, said individual charitable acts are not prohibited under China's Charity Law, although he acknowledged that free services could affect businesses depending on the circumstances.

Beijing News reported that a noodle shop about 100 meters from one of the outlets saw its daily turnover fall by about 200 yuan ($30) to 300 yuan after the Zero-Yuan Noodle Shop opened. Its owner, whose lease expires in March, plans to relocate, citing the free shop's presence nearby.

Another noodle shop about 800 meters away reported being less affected. Its owner said the free shop mainly attracted customers who would normally buy low-priced noodles, as well as elderly people who had time to wait in line.

Even so, the owner said he plans to relocate when his lease expires, as nearby noodle shops may lower their prices to survive, drawing them into a price war.

In contrast, another nearby noodle shop owner said turnover was still rising, crediting a stable base of regular patrons.

Zhang told the Yangtse Evening Post that the shop's single-item menu set it apart from nearby restaurants, which cater to a wider range of tastes and budgets. He said his shops could not start charging customers because the name had already ruled out that possibility. He added that no profit model had been designed so far.

Huang said helping people in need, including seniors who have no one at home to cook for them, was "definitely a charitable act".

"No law says that his charitable act is wrong, nor does the law prohibit him from doing it," he said.

"Doing charity is a citizen's right. It is something that should be encouraged, and the central government does encourage it."

Huang acknowledged that charitable acts may affect ordinary businesses when they suddenly enter a normal market. However, he said the impact cannot be judged in the abstract and must be assessed in light of multiple factors.

On Nov 12, Red Star News reported that a staff member with the Jiangning district market supervision administration in Nanjing said that an on-site inspection of the "zero-yuan" noodle shop had been carried out earlier. The shop has all required licenses and permits, and all spot checks of its food ingredients passed. "To date, no violations of laws or regulations in the field of market regulation have been found at the noodle shop," the staff member said.
