New soybean variety could lessen import reliance.

A homegrown soybean variety has achieved a yield of 5,573 kilograms per hectare in a recent field trial in Northeast China, marking a breakthrough in efforts to boost domestic soybean production and reduce the country's reliance on imports.

The variety, named Dongsheng 89, developed by a research team at Guangzhou University in Guangdong province, has delivered yields more than 2.7 times the national average in demonstrations for two consecutive years, according to the team.

The latest yield test was conducted in Suiling county, Heilongjiang province, where the variety was harvested from farmers' fields rather than experimental plots, providing evidence of its performance under real-world planting conditions.

Dongsheng 89 has been tested on about 10,000 hectares in Northeast China, with farmers participating in commercial production. The demonstration fields vary in soil conditions and farming practices, making the results particularly significant for assessing the variety's stability and potential for wider adoption, researchers said.

The breakthrough stems from a different approach to soybean breeding, which focused on maximizing yields through dense planting rather than increasing the productivity of individual plants, said Dong Lidong, a professor on the soybean research team at Guangzhou University.

Traditional soybean varieties tend to become vulnerable to lodging, or falling over, when planting density increases, limiting their potential for higher yields.

Dongsheng 89 features a compact, short-stemmed structure that allows more plants to grow in a given area without significantly compromising their pod-bearing capacity.

The team identified a key gene, RIN1, which shortens the distance between stem nodes while maintaining the number of nodes, making the plants better suited to high-density cultivation.

The gene was discovered through mutation breeding. Researchers screened a population of about 200,000 mutant plants to identify a promising individual, which was then propagated through successive generations to establish a stable, inheritable trait.

The resulting variety is non-GMO and contains 42 percent protein and 20.8 percent oil, according to the research team.

The team has demonstrated the variety's stability through large-scale field trials, with 51.3 hectares planted in 2025 and 110 hectares in 2026, both recording yields exceeding 5,565 kg per hectare.

"By increasing the number of plants per unit area and making better use of the collective effect of the crop population, we have found a viable way to increase soybean yields," Dong said.

Under comparable selling prices, growers could earn an additional 3,000 to 4,000 yuan ($449 to $599) per hectare, he said.

The variety's promotion has been included in the first batch of 136 landmark application-scenario projects announced by government departments, which aim to accelerate the adoption of new technologies and products through real-world applications.

The team plans to expand demonstrations to 66,667 hectares before scaling up further. It is also developing new compact, high-density soybean materials adapted to other major growing regions through crossbreeding and molecular marker-assisted breeding.

China produces about 20 million metric tons of soybeans annually but imports more than 100 million tons to meet domestic demand, making the crop a major source of the country's dependence on imports.

"If varieties such as Dongsheng 89 could be widely adopted nationwide, soybean production could increase substantially without expanding acreage, potentially lifting domestic output to about 60 million tons and the self-sufficiency rate to around 50 percent," Dong said.
