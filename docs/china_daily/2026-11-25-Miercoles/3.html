CAR-T expertise draws foreign cancer patients.

A Romanian patient with a serious form of lymphoma, a type of blood cancer that had returned despite previous treatment, has been discharged from Shanghai Tongji Hospital after receiving an advanced cancer treatment administered by a Chinese medical team.

The 58-year-old woman, Sandica-Iuliana Klein, came to Shanghai specifically for treatment this summer. Doctors said she was the hospital's first Romanian patient and may also be the first Romanian to travel to China for CAR-T therapy, an advanced treatment that uses a patient's own immune cells to fight cancer.

CAR-T therapy involves collecting immune cells called T cells from a patient's blood, modifying them in a laboratory to help them recognize and attack cancer cells, and then returning them to the patient's body. The treatment essentially equips the body's natural defense system with a more targeted ability to fight tumors.

Klein underwent a combination of radiotherapy and CAR-T therapy at the Shanghai hospital. By the time she was discharged, her levels of lactate dehydrogenase, an enzyme in the blood that can indicate the extent of tumor activity, had fallen significantly, suggesting a positive response to treatment.

Klein had previously received treatment for lymphoma in Romania and achieved remission, meaning the signs of cancer had disappeared. However, the disease progressed again last year. Further chemotherapy in Romania brought only partial remission, with some cancer remaining.

With CAR-T treatment unavailable in her home country, long waiting times in many other countries and relatively few hospitals in Europe offering comprehensive treatment, Klein and her family began looking overseas. They eventually focused on China after learning about Tongji Hospital's experience in CAR-T therapy and its successful treatment of international patients.

Through an online medical consultation, they contacted the team led by Li Ping, a professor in the hospital's hematology department. Klein arrived in Shanghai on Aug 22, and her T cells were collected six days later.

Li said that she also had scleroderma, a rare condition that causes the skin to become hard and tight, as well as hypothyroidism, in which the thyroid gland does not produce enough hormones to regulate functions such as metabolism.

"Using CAR-T treatment for her was like killing multiple birds with one stone, and it really saved her life," Li said.

The medical team conducted additional genetic tests and found a mutation that placed Klein at a higher risk of the cancer returning. To reduce that risk, doctors arranged radiotherapy before reinfusing her modified T cells. The aim was to shrink the tumor first, making the subsequent CAR-T treatment more effective and reducing the likelihood of relapse, Li said.

"Often the challenge with CAR-T lies not in the reinfusion itself but in the comprehensive management before and after the infusion," she said, emphasizing the importance of careful preparation and follow-up.

Klein, whose husband is from the United States, said patients in her home country usually consider seeking advanced treatment in North America. However, she decided on China after comparing treatment costs and medical experience.

CAR-T treatment in China costs about one-third of the price in the US, while Chinese hospitals also have extensive experience in administering the therapy, she said.

Tongji Hospital was among the first hospitals in China to conduct clinical research on CAR-T therapy, beginning in 2015. The hospital has since treated more than 1,000 patients. More than 100 patients from foreign countries and regions, including Thailand and Singapore, have traveled to the Shanghai hospital specifically for the treatment.

"What surprised me the most was how international this hospital is. From the first video call with Professor Li, I felt I could entrust myself to this team. They are professional and gentle, explaining every step clearly, even those test results I failed to understand," Klein said.

Following her discharge, Li's team will conduct monthly remote follow-ups for six months and coordinate with hospitals in Romania to arrange further care.
