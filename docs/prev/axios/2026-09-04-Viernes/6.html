Record 47% of Americans want more powerful unions.

For only the second time in a quarter century, a majority of Republicans — 52% — approve of labor unions , according to a new Gallup poll .

Why it matters: Despite Americans' rising approval , union membership remains near a modern low. Congress has repeatedly blocked labor's biggest priorities, and House Republicans are now investigating unions, even as their own voters approve of them.

By the numbers: Overall, 71% of Americans approved of unions, tying the 2022 level. Gallup last recorded higher approval in 1959 at 73%.

The big picture: The latest numbers mark a reversal from the Great Recession. In 2009, union approval bottomed out at 48%, and 42% of Americans wanted unions to have less influence while just 25% wanted them to have more.

Reality check: Americans are embracing unions at a time when few workers belong to one. Just 11% of U.S. workers — 16.5 million — were represented by unions in 2025, compared with 23% and 20.5 million workers in 1983, the first year of comparable data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics .

State of play: Workers have been trying to make up for lost time. Union election petitions filed with the National Labor Relations Board more than doubled from 2021 to 3,286 in 2024. The agency received 2,633 election petitions last year , down from the prior year's peak but still well above earlier years.

Zoom in: Organized labor has repeatedly failed to turn Democratic control of Washington into its biggest legislative prize: rewriting federal law to make it easier for workers to join unions and to win contracts.

Between the lines: President Biden leaned on executive action and appointments, creating a task force to promote organizing and pushing the NLRB to favor unionizing workers. In 2023, he became the first president to join striking workers on a picket line, standing with autoworkers in Michigan.

The latest: House Republicans have also intensified scrutiny of union leadership, launching investigations into the United Auto Workers, United Steelworkers, the American Federation of Teachers and three railroad unions, demanding records about political activity.

The bottom line: Labor has won back popularity it lost during decades of decline, but its harder fight is turning that goodwill into bargaining power and growing its membership.

The fine print: Gallup surveyed 1,200 U.S. adults by cellphone and landline Aug. 3–24. The overall margin of error is 4 percentage points.
