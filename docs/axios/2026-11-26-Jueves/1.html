The pros and cons of Medicare for All.

The boldest idea in health care comes down to one massive tradeoff: Guaranteed coverage for everyone in exchange for an eye-popping price tag and an ultra-messy transition.

Why it matters: Talk of Medicare for All is growing now that Democrats will control Congress next year with huge majorities in both chambers — so it's time to take a clinical look at the pros and cons, based on what we're hearing from policy experts who have studied it.

Driving the news: The idea is getting a fresh round of attention after Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed (D-Mich.) — a major supporter of Medicare for All who wrote a book about it — offered to name the plan "TrumpCare," complete with gold insurance cards, if President Trump helps pass it.

Reality check: They will probably meet, and it could end with mutual praise and hopeful vibes (with echoes of Trump's meetings with New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani). El-Sayed likes to flatter Trump. Trump likes to be flattered.

But Democrats can certainly try to force votes on Medicare for All when they're in power, although they're likely to find that not everyone in the party is on board.

The pros: Medicare for All would replace the nation's fragmented insurance system with universal coverage that's subject to a single standard.

The cons: Sticker shock. The projected 10-year cost of implementing a plan is about $47 trillion, per the White House Council of Economic Advisers, which predicts higher taxes, more deficit spending and program cuts.

Between the lines: Some Democrats are already convinced they're not going to get Medicare for All, so they're looking into narrower changes like lowering the eligibility age for Medicare — although even those ideas could cause headaches of their own .

The bottom line: There's no such thing as "problem solved" in health care — but the Democratic sweep could give voice to the voters who are tired of the current problems and want to try something new.
