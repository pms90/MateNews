Why it matters: We write this neither to forecast nor frighten. Rather, we're sharing what government and AI leaders say privately but won't say publicly.

The big picture: The disruptions of the past two weeks — from a global Microsoft outage to the FAA grounding air travel to a consumer banking glitch at Chase — were a mild preview of what experts expect is coming.

Horacio Rozanski , chairman and CEO of Booz Allen, calls this the "fog before the war" — a foretaste of greater chaos to come, when agentic invaders reach their full potential and can attack "at the speed of thought."

Most of these malfunctions appear to have resulted from companies and government trying to secure their computer and infrastructure systems against future large-scale cyberattacks. This pattern will persist.

The best-case scenario described to us: regular, random outages and small-scale scares like losing bank access or air travel for a day.

What worries top officials most : With thin AI expertise, an understaffed government and major companies are trying to keep pace with a technology that improves daily.

Think of it as whack-a-mole with infinite moles.

Step back: Only by understanding the state of AI + cyber can government and business mobilize to prevent possible shutdowns across critical banking, electricity and water systems. This is our crack at distilling our conversations in a digestible way for Axios readers:

The reasons not to freak out: Everyone we talked to expects attacks. Few predict true catastrophe. There's a big gap between a bank going out for a day and a region going dark for a week.

So what could be done? The solution sits with the very first point of our distillation: We have the tech to both cause and fix this cyber threat. The government, through leverage or mandate, could pull the AI companies together and force them to help fortify vulnerable American systems before focusing fully on simply building more powerful models.

Jay Clayton , chair of the White House task force, is finishing a voluntary safety regime for the big AI companies and is probably best positioned to pull AI CEOs together. He's well-versed in cyber and sees AI largely through a national security lens.

The bottom line: When we press all these leaders across government, business and AI, they agree that the chances of working together to prevent an attack are lower than the chances of an actual attack.
