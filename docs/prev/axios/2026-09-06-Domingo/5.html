5. Republicans sour on the economy.

Republicans' confidence in the economy has plummeted over the last six months at a pace unseen since Covid — reminiscent of the declines during the global financial crisis nearly two decades ago.

Why it matters: Usually, the president's party is upbeat about the economy, while the party out of power is far more downcast. What's striking now is that Republicans have grown increasingly sour this year, even with a Republican president in office.

By the numbers: Since February, Republican consumer sentiment has plunged 15.1 index points, from 97.1 to 82, according to the latest University of Michigan Surveys of Consumers data. Democratic sentiment has fallen just 1.6 index points, from 41.8 to 40.2.

Flashback: It's rare for one party's outlook to remain steady while the other's falls — typically they move in tandem. But a similar split occurred during the global financial crisis in late 2008 and early 2009.

Caveat: That period included the election and inauguration of a Democratic president, accounting for some of the Republican decline.

What they're saying: "The vast majority of the time, Republicans and Democrats move broadly in parallel," Joanne Hsu, director of the Surveys of Consumers, told Axios. But that is not the case now.

Yes, but: Hsu said it's important to remember that, despite the slide, Republicans still have a much more positive outlook on the economy than Democrats.

Zoom in: The Michigan survey tracks with other data on how Republicans feel about the economy under Trump, including a July Pew Research poll .

The other side: "The hard data of consumer spending and retail sales have, for months, consistently reinforced that the American consumer remains resilient thanks to the President's proven economic agenda of tax cuts and deregulation," said White House spokesman Kush Desai.

The bottom line: It's no secret that the election will turn on the economy. Inflation remains untamed, and the war in Iran shows no sign of ending.
