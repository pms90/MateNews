Los campos de trabajo refuerzan la conservación del patrimonio.

GUIYANG — Durante más de una década, los voluntarios internacionales han sido una presencia familiar cada verano en la antigua aldea de Yunshan Tun en la ciudad de Anshun, provincia de Guizhou. Este sitio histórico, marcado por fortalezas militares de 600 años de antigüedad, alberga un grupo de ciudadelas de piedra conocidas como Tunpu.

Este verano, un grupo de estudiantes, empresarios y arquitectos franceses cambiaron sus escritorios y estudios por herramientas de construcción y trabajaron junto a artesanos locales para quitar tejas viejas, limpiar paredes dañadas y transportar pesadas vigas de madera para ayudar a restaurar casas centenarias.

Hasta la fecha, 429 voluntarios de China, Francia, Grecia, el Reino Unido, Noruega, Bélgica, Australia, Alemania y otros países se han unido a los campos de trabajo de conservación de las casas antiguas, incluidos 98 voluntarios extranjeros, dijo Yue Jian, arquitecto de Guizhou y organizador de los campamentos junto con REMPART, una red francesa de voluntarios para la conservación del patrimonio.

Durante su estadía de dos semanas, o más, en cada campamento, el grupo trabajó codo con codo con artesanos locales y equipos de construcción en tareas físicas exigentes y adquirió experiencia práctica en raras habilidades artesanales tradicionales. Cincuenta voluntarios franceses participaron en el campo de trabajo de Tunpu. Entre los participantes de este año se encontraba Fatoumata Camara, de 25 años, estudiante del Instituto Nacional de Lenguas y Civilizaciones Orientales de Francia, que regresó a China en agosto para su segunda experiencia de conservación.

"Había oído a voluntarios franceses anteriores que este era uno de los campos de trabajo más antiguos entre Francia y China. Saber que tanta gente había formado parte de él a lo largo de los años me hizo querer contribuir también", dijo Camara mientras describió la experiencia como "comprender la historia de China en lugares donde la gente todavía vive y donde las tradiciones todavía están vivas".

Antes de su llegada, Camara suponía que las casas tradicionales del sur de China serían predominantemente de madera. En cambio, encontró un uso extensivo de muros de piedra combinados con estructuras de madera, un legado arquitectónico de la historia militar de la región.

Yunshan Tun es parte de los distintivos asentamientos Tunpu de Guizhou, un grupo de aldeas fortaleza militares que datan de unos seis siglos. Construidas durante la dinastía Ming (1368-1644), las guarniciones albergaban tropas, cultivaban tierras de cultivo y custodiaban la frontera suroeste de China.

La mayoría de los asentamientos se concentran alrededor de Anshun, a menos de 100 kilómetros de la capital provincial, Guiyang. El uso extensivo de piedra le valió a la zona el apodo de "reino de la piedra".

La casa que Camara ayudó a restaurar cubre 128 metros cuadrados en dos pisos y está ubicada en el sitio de un puesto militar de la dinastía Ming. "Me mostró hasta dónde puede llegar un proyecto de conservación del patrimonio a largo plazo y cuánto se puede transformar una aldea con el tiempo", afirmó.

Preservando casas antiguas

En las últimas décadas, una amplia cooperación entre instituciones chinas y francesas ha llevado a conservadores internacionales a reparar estructuras antiguas (templos, muros y casas) no sólo en Guizhou, sino también en varios sitios, incluidos aquellos en Pingyao, en la provincia de Shanxi, y en la provincia de Fujian. El campo de trabajo de Pingyao ha sido incluido en la iniciativa Voluntarios del Patrimonio Mundial de la UNESCO. En Guizhou, el programa ha ayudado a reparar 13 casas centenarias.

Yue Jian, who initiated the volunteer project in Tunpu villages in 2014, visited the stone citadels in the 1990s when he was still an architecture student. Fascinated by the local approach to building in harmony with the terrain, Yue developed a deep commitment to preserving these historical structures. He followed advice from Ruan Yisan, a renowned Chinese expert on the preservation of historical cities, to work as a volunteer first in France.

"I discovered that heritage preservation did not have to be the exclusive domain of experts and professional institutions. Ordinary young people could also participate, working under professional guidance while learning about architecture and traditional craftsmanship," he said.

Back in Guizhou, he began exploring ways to adapt this approach to rural China. "The French work camps often focus on large medieval castles, while traditional villages in Guizhou consist of numerous smaller residences, each with different owners and users," he said. Yue treated the entire village as "one large castle", with each traditional house forming part of it.

In French villages, volunteers learned traditional stone-cutting and masonry techniques in restoring medieval stone castles damaged by war. In Guizhou's Tunpu villages, French participants have followed local carpenters to learn traditional timber-frame construction and use indigenous tools such as ink lines, hand planes and chisels to work on wooden columns and joints. "We went to France to learn stonework, and they came to Guizhou to learn woodworking," Yue said.

For participants like Camara, the restoration did not attempt to freeze the village in the past, but aimed to create a balance between preserving the old and bringing in something new that could attract visitors and support the village. "Conservation is not only about making a place beautiful for visitors, but making sure that people living in these historical places have standards of comfort and hygiene," she said.

The work camp also offered activities in local traditional arts, such as Tunpu opera, batik and wooden mask carving. "I've learnt much more than just restoration techniques. They showed me how incredibly diverse China is. Every region has its own traditions, cuisine, and landscape," she said.

The current repair project, funded by an architecture firm in Shanghai, is scheduled to be completed in August 2027. Local conservation efforts are also increasing. The Anshun city culture and tourism authorities have invested more than 230 million yuan ($34 million) in restoring 27 key heritage sites in Tunpu villages and used 3D scanning to digitally document 1,187 immovable cultural heritage sites and 307 items of intangible cultural heritage, forming a digital resource database for Tunpu culture.

Deep cultural pull

Marie-Georges Pagel-Brousse, president of REMPART, has traveled to China 15 times, but one particular encounter with a Guizhou foreman was the most memorable. At a work camp in Xijiang Miao village in Guizhou in 2019, a local craftsman showed how China's ancestral techniques can help reduce the use of electric power tools. "Guizhou's traditional joinery and carpentry techniques became a lesson for French volunteers. Here traditions have fortunately not vanished; they remain vital, especially where craft knowhow is concerned," she said.

For Pagel-Brousse, the work camps demonstrated the value of public participation in heritage conservation. "The presence of volunteers can sometimes encourage local people to rediscover the value of traditions that have become part of their everyday lives," she said.

"The volunteers gain exposure to different yet inspiring working approaches, and develop deep respect for local masters who command their techniques to perfection. Chinese architecture is so impressive and remarkable that it leaves a lasting, uplifting impression," she said. "The French people I know who have been to China feel a strong pull toward this country and its culture. They travel such a long distance because they are eager to learn, to meet others and to share with people from different backgrounds. None have ever left disappointed."

After the construction team finishes and heads out, it's up to the local residents to keep looking after the old houses, Yue said. "Repairing historical houses represents merely the initial stage. Finding ways for them to remain part of modern life poses a new issue to address," he said.

Guizhou has carried out systematic surveys of the remains of military and residential settlements with preliminary documentation completed for more than 300 Tunpu villages, covering their history, customs and cultural practices. The scope of conservation has also expanded from individual houses to entire village layouts, traditional craftsmanship, intangible cultural heritage and the everyday lives of residents.

Some restored houses have become tour venues, heritage workshops, cafes and homestays. During the 2025 Mid-Autumn Festival and National Day holiday period, a peak travel season, five major Tunpu scenic areas in Anshun received more than 1.4 million tourists, generating tourism revenue of over 69 million yuan. Camara noticed the change during her stay. Many old houses have been returned to use as homestays, restaurants and cultural spaces. Modern sanitary facilities coexist with traditional architecture, while residents continue to live in the village and traditional costumes and festivals remain part of local life.
